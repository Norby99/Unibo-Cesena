# Introduction
This report presents the implementation of a footbot control system in ARGoS based on the Motor Schemas architecture. The objective is to design reactive behaviors that guide the robot toward a light source as quickly as possible while ensuring effective obstacle avoidance within the arena.

# Implementation
## Conceptual Design
First, the following fundamental behaviors were defined:

- Follow: Drives the robot toward light sources when no imminent danger is detected. It utilizes a **Uniform** potential field to guide the robot toward the target.

- Fear: Reacts to proximity sensors to steer the robot away from walls and obstacles using a **Repulsive** potential field.

- StopAtDarkSpot: Ensures the robot stops immediately when a dark spot or hole is detected, fulfilling the primary safety constraint and preventing the robot from falling or leaving the target area.

- RandomWander: Makes the robot follow a random direction for a given number of steps using a **Uniform** potential field to break deadlocks.

A static weighting mechanism was introduced for the various behaviors. This ensures that less critical behaviors do not override the more important ones during vector summation.

## Halting at the Target
The chosen approach involved implementing a new behavior called StopAtDarkSpot. This behavior returns nil if the ground is white, and a valid vector otherwise. To handle the halting mechanism, every behavior implements a method called `should_halt` that accepts a list of monitored behaviors. If any of the behaviors in this list returns a non nil value, the current behavior will also return nil, thereby nullifying its contribution to the final vector.

An alternative approach would be to not stop the robot completely, but rather design a behavior that applies a Perpetual or **Attractive** potential field when the robot is close to the light source and positioned over the dark spot. However, with this method, the robot would still maintain some degree of movement instead of coming to a full halt.

# Observations
## Behavior Adjustments
Regarding the Follow behavior, using an **Attractive** potential field proved ineffective when a wall was located close to the light source, prompting a switch to a **Uniform** field. Because the intensity of an attractive field decreases as the robot approaches the target, the repulsive field generated by Fear became dominant near walls. Consequently, the robot tended to drift away from the target instead of reaching it.

The RandomWander behavior was introduced not for environmental exploration—which was already implicitly handled by the sensor noise affecting the Follow behavior—but rather to resolve specific local minima. For instance, when obstacles were placed near a wall, the robot could get trapped in a dead lock. Adding random wander injected enough noise to break the robot out of these loops.

## Advantages and Disadvantages of the Motor Schema Architecture
- Simplicity of Output: Unlike the Subsumption architecture, the use of vectors greatly simplifies motor control. By looking at the final summed vector, the robot's intended direction of travel is immediately clear.

- Ease of Interconnection: It is much easier to understand and implement the interconnections between different behaviors. In the Subsumption architecture, behaviors are explicitly chained together, requiring inspection of the overriding behavior's internal logic. This often leads to code duplication across modules, which Motor Schemas avoid.

- Potential for a Hybrid Approach: An interesting improvement would be to implement a hybrid approach between the two architectures. This would involve retaining the Subsumption architecture's strict priority system—preventing issues where a random walk inadvertently steers the robot in the wrong direction—while keeping the Motor Schemas' core concept of behaviors outputting continuous potential fields.

## Architectural Limitations and Alternative Design Considerations
During the testing phase, a performance limitation was identified regarding the combination of normalized vectors and static weights. Since every behavior outputs a vector with an intensity bounded between 0 and 1, and these are subsequently multiplied by static weights, the robot's overall velocity drops drastically in open space. When the robot is far from both obstacles and light sources, only the RandomWander behavior is active. However, because its static weight must remain low to prevent it from overriding critical behaviors, the resulting summed vector is extremely small, causing the robot to move inefficiently slow.

To resolve this issue, an alternative optimization based on dynamic gains and situational confidence was evaluated. In this proposed framework, each behavior would consistently output a unit vector (intensity equal to 1), which would then be scaled dynamically by a "gain" coefficient proportional to the robot's environmental perception. Under this framework, static weights would become redundant, and the explicit halting mechanism (`should_halt`) could be bypassed by simply applying an overridingly high gain to the target behavior when the dark spot is detected.

While this dynamic gain approach would significantly optimize exploration speed, it was ultimately excluded from the final implementation, because deviates substantially from the pure, continuous vector-summation principles inherent to the Motor Schemas architecture requested by the assignment.

# Performance Evaluation
## Statistical Framework
To ensure a sound statistical estimation of the Motor Schemas controller, the system was evaluated over 40 independent simulation trials. In each run, the footbot's initial position and orientation within the ARGoS arena were completely randomized. The maximum simulation window was capped at 1,500 steps, and the performance was quantified using two primary metrics: the Task Completion Time (measured in control steps) and the overall Success Rate (%).

## Experimental Results
The quantitative data gathered from the experimental phase yielded the following statistical breakdown:

- Success Rate: The robot achieved a 90% success rate (36 out of 40 trials), successfully navigating to the light source and halting on the dark spot.

- Navigation Efficiency: Among the successful trials, the robot required an average of 1,025 steps to complete the task, with a standard deviation of 145 steps.

- Failure Analysis: In the remaining 10% of trials (4 runs), the robot failed to reach the target before the 1,500 step timeout. In these cases, the robot became trapped in the previously mentioned local minima caused by symmetrical obstacle configurations, where the repulsive forces of the walls exactly counteracted the attractive forces of the light source, rendering the minor perturbations of the RandomWander behavior insufficient to break the equilibrium.

## Comparative Analysis: Motor Schemas vs. Subsumption
When compared to the previously implemented Subsumption Architecture, the Motor Schemas configuration exhibited significantly worse performance across all key metrics. While the Subsumption controller guided the robot to the target in an average of approximately 710 steps, the Motor Schemas architecture required roughly 45% more time, averaging over 1,000 steps.

This performance degradation can be attributed to two main structural factors inherent to vector-summation mechanics:

1. The Open-Space Velocity Bottleneck: As detailed in the architectural limitations, the reliance on static weights severely penalized the robot's linear speed. In open spaces far from obstacles, the final summed velocity vector remained exceptionally small, causing the robot to spend a massive number of steps crawling toward the light source.

2. Vector Cancellation and Suboptimal Trajectories: Unlike the sharp, decisive behavioral switches of the Subsumption architecture, the Motor Schemas architecture continuously blends forces. When navigating near walls, the repulsive vector from the Fear behavior partially canceled out the forward momentum of the Follow behavior. Instead of cleanly skirting around an obstacle at full speed, the robot executed a wide, sluggish, and wavy trajectory, drastically inflating the overall step count.
